About

I'm driven by curiosity more than anything else. I started out as a mechanical engineer, and I never lost the need to know how a thing works one layer down. These days that curiosity points at AI, and at one moment in particular: when a system stops answering and starts acting for someone. What makes a person comfortable handing over that next step is the question I care about most.

I learn by building. Hand me ten articles and I'll probably understand the idea, but for me the practical is where the rubber meets the road. It's what keeps me up late, talking with Claude and other agents, following a question down the rabbit hole until I've actually found out what's at the bottom of it.

How I Think

From first principles

The engineering habit stuck. I don't fully trust a system I can't explain from the layer beneath it, which is why I've gone as far down as how a processor turns electricity into a running program.

In probabilities

A confident answer is only worth something if the confidence means something. I'd rather be wrong in a way I can measure than right in a way I can't explain.

Brainstorm, then build

I think out loud first, with people and with agents, until the shape of an idea shows up. Then I build the smallest version that can prove me wrong. The cost of finding out has collapsed, so the working thing is usually faster than the argument.

Questions I Keep Coming Back To

When has a system earned the right to act?

Autonomy isn't a setting. It's earned per action. Reading a report and changing a price cost very different amounts when the model is wrong, so they deserve different thresholds.

What happens when it's wrong?

Every AI product is partly a recovery product. People forgive a wrong answer far more easily than one they couldn't catch, undo, or get a human to fix.

What does an intent-based interface look like?

A terminal assumes you know the command, a wizard assumes someone pre-decided the steps, and a chat window turns everything into a transcript. None of them fit software that generates its own interface, which is why I'm following A2UI closely.

What changes when voice is the primary interface?

I think it's coming. The problems it raises, like who's speaking, what they changed their mind about, and what to do when you only half heard, are ones every ambient interface will have to solve.

Where My Free Time Goes

Experimenting to keep up

The space moves faster than anyone can read about it, so I keep up the way I learn best: by trying things. Claude Code, OpenClaw, Goose, different frontier models on the same problem. Most experiments start as a brainstorm and end as something small that works. What I learn goes into a wiki structured so an agent can read it as well as I can.

Learning how things work from the silicon up

A long-running path through semiconductors and chip design. The interactive explainers on processors and transformers came out of it: I build them to check I actually understand something.

A few unfinished takes

Some rabbit holes don't end in a finished project, and that's fine. Right now there are a couple open: how prediction markets price uncertainty, and whether old mathematical philosophy has anything to teach modern markets. I keep coming back to them.

Agentic commerce, as a curiosity

Agent protocols can already tell you who an agent is. Almost nothing tells you whether it's any good. I think reputation, not identity, is what will decide whether people let an agent spend their money.
